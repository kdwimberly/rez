Chapter One

ZEVORA I

Scroll to uncover ↓

Zevora Daevor had always despised the home she grew up in.

Not for its jagged walls or vacant rooms. No, she hated it because she had grown up inside them, because every stone seemed to remember who she was expected to be. It was a fortress first and a home only by habit, simply a monument to dominance meant to smother softness; yet softness was one lesson she never unlearned.

Colossal, there it stood above Daesmarch like a hulking titan, black stone rising from the earth in jagged spires and bastioned walls. Its construction was an intricate design of iron and geometry, a stronghold built to endure the ravages of both war and time.

Few would call it beautiful; its grandeur did not inspire awe so much as submission. It was a monument to unrelenting strength, cold and absolute, carved from the very mountains that held the city within their grasp.

At dawn, its silhouette cast long, grasping fingers across the grid-lined streets below, as though to remind the people that the Daevors’ dominion was inescapable, forever present.

The dining hall where Zevora sat mirrored the fortress around it—vast and grim, adorned with the spoils of forgotten wars. Greatswords, axes, hammers, shields, spears, they all lined the walls, each polished to an immaculate gleam. Brass pipes and copper tubing snaked across the ceiling like metallic vines, occasionally releasing hisses of steam through ornate valves.

Between them, crystalline glyphs pulsed with ethereal light, their ancient symbols casting an otherworldly orange glow that danced off the polished gears and clockwork mechanisms adorning the walls. Ancient banners hung like the skins of conquered beasts, their symbols dimmed and rugged with age, now framed by intricate brass fixtures.

Between tall windows of smoky glass, statues of long-dead Daevors stared down with sightless, domineering eyes, their stone features frozen in the certainty of their own righteousness.

The table, black obsidian and unblemished, almost spanned the length of the room, though only three figures sat within its empty expanse. The Daevors dined the same way they ruled, encircled by silence and a plethora of space.

Zevora sat still as stone, her back straight, her expression composed. Her father, Aldrick Daevor, the Head of their Family, occupied the seat at the far end, where the light cast cruel shadows across his unemotional face. He was a man carved from the same rock that housed their fortress—large, dark, bald, and unyielding, his old age hidden beneath the hard edges of his brow and jaw. Every movement was slow, deliberate. Even now, as he carved through a joint of meat with mechanical efficiency, his hands as steady as a surgeon.

To his left, Natalia Daevor, his wife and Zevora’s mother, moved with a grace that bordered on unnatural. Her gown was a river of dark silk, pooling around her as though she might sink into the chair and become one with it. Her dark-brown hair was drawn back in intricate braids woven with silver, blending in with her grey strands, and her skin, porcelain, seemed untouched by the passing of time. Natalia was beautiful, yes, but hers was the beauty of glass: flawless, polished, and cold. A single wrong word would shatter her composure, and she would cut you as she broke.

In the middle of the table, in the seat she had claimed since childhood, Zevora felt like a shadow, neither as commanding as her father nor as polished as her mother. She wore a simple dress of beige, its lack of ornamentation a quiet defiance in a house that draped itself in wealth. Her wavy dark-brown hair, tips golden from the sun, hung loosely to her shoulders, refusing the elaborate styles her mother preferred—it was too thick anyway. At her neck, a small pendant hung on a silver chain—its glyph barely visible, etched with a quiet grace.

Aldrick’s voice broke the silence, deep and clipped.

“Dameon has outdone himself.”

The words shattered the brittle quiet like a hammer striking stone. Zevora’s fingers curled into fists in her lap.

Oh, here we go.

She braced the way she had as a child—chin lifted just enough to appear composed, heart tucked away where they couldn’t reach it.

“His performance in the arena the other night was as flawless as ever,” her father continued, setting down his knife with a clink. “The Assembly could scarcely speak of anything else yesterday. A crowd is a fickle monster, but he knows how to bend them to his will. He understands what they need. An actual champion.”

Zevora always wondered if her father saw Dameon as a son or as proof that the Daevor bloodline could still forge something sharp enough to draw blood.

“What they want,” Natalia corrected, her voice soft and as cool as a flowing brook. “Entertainment. A hero to cheer for, distraction from the rot eating through their minds. Dameon has given them that and more.”

Zevora glanced between them. Her father, dark-eyed and stern, speaking of her brother like a general admiring his finest weapon. Her mother, calm and self-assured, as though Dameon’s triumph was her own.

And perhaps it was. They had molded him as surely as the Daevors had helped mold Daesmarch.

“The next bout will be his most impressive yet,” Aldrick said, leaning back in his chair. The flickering glyphlight caught the sharp planes of his face, casting shadows that exaggerated his already severe features. “I know it. That pesky Magistrate of Edgenear has sent something they call a champion to the arena—a veteran of no less than a dozen campaigns. A crowd-pleaser. He’s expected to put on quite the show before Dameon cuts him down. He’s got a mouth on him, I’ve heard. Only a bonus.”

Natalia smiled faintly, folding her hands in her lap. “A battle of unmatched skill. The people will love it.”

Zevora’s jaw tightened. She could already hear the echo of the crowd roar in her mind, their bloodlust reverberating through the arena as her brother delivered yet another “perfect” kill.

“And Dameon will do as he always does,” she said quietly. “Give them their show and leave the sand red for their applause.”

Her father’s gaze sharpened. “And you will attend this time.”

Of course. As if I have no option.

Zevora’s hands clenched beneath the table. She wasn’t defiant by nature. She simply refused to worship what everyone else called entertainment.

“No,” she retaliated.

Natalia’s brow arched, her expression almost amused. “You refuse? Again?”

Zevora looked to her mother, her voice calm but firm. “Yes, Mother. Again. I will not sit and cheer for the slaughter of another man just to feed the Forum’s delusions of power.”

“Delusions?” Aldrick’s voice cut through the room like a blade. “You call it delusion to command respect? To unify the provinces under the banner of power? Those sands are where history is made, Zevora. The Forum cheers because they see the future of what true leadership looks like.”

“Leadership? Like mother said, they cheer because it distracts them from the weight of their own lives,” Zevora countered. “Because they’ve been taught to believe that bloodshed is glory and that violence is strength. But there’s no glory in it, Father. Only death.”

Natalia’s expression hardened, the faint amusement vanishing. “Do you truly think so little of your brother’s achievements?”

Did I ever say that?

Zevora faltered. Dameon—for all his arrogance, all his ruthless precision, he had once been her brother in truth—a boy who had climbed trees with her in the hills above Daesmarch. The only one who could make her laugh until she cried. Though, that boy seemed so far away now, buried beneath the polished mask their parents had crafted for him.

“I think,” Zevora said carefully, “that Dameon doesn’t need me there to win another meaningless bout. He has the Forum, or more importantly, the Assembly and the entire city behind him—or what, practically all of the provinces? What difference would my presence make?”

“Optics,” Aldrick said flatly. “Your brother is not just another Vanguard; he is becoming the face of this family. Every victory brings us closer to securing the Chancellor’s seat. A united Daevor front strengthens that image. Do you not see the value in that?”

“I see the value you place on appearances,” Zevora replied, her voice edged now. “And you speak of unity as if it were real. Let me know, Father, how does that unity extend to Zarick?”

The name hung in the air like a blade poised to strike.

Aldrick’s jaw tightened, words barely slipping through his teeth. “Your brother chose to leave. He made his own decisions.”

Zevora’s gaze didn’t waver. “He left because he saw what you were making him into. He couldn’t bear to be your pawn, so he refused to play.”

“Cowardice,” Aldrick said dismissively. “He abandoned his duty to the family.”

“Or perhaps he chose a different kind of duty,” Zevora said quietly.

Natalia sighed, picking an invisible speck from her sleeve. “Enough of this. Zarick is not the topic of discussion.” Her tone was light, but the edge beneath it was unmistakable. “We are speaking of Dameon, and of what must be done. Zevora, your presence at the arena is not simply a request—it is an expectation. Your brother’s success benefits us all, and the family’s image must be maintained.”

Zevora’s chest tightened. She could feel the walls closing in around her, the weight of their expectations pressing down like iron chains. She pushed her chair back, the sound scraping through the air like a rebuke. “If Dameon’s strength is so unassailable, then it shouldn’t need to lean on me,” she said, rising to her feet.

She paused for a moment, catching herself.

“I love Dameon,” her voice softer, more sincere now, “but that does not mean I agree with what he has become. I will not support what I do not believe in.”

Aldrick’s gaze burned into her, unflinching. “Your beliefs are irrelevant.”

“Perhaps to you,” Zevora said, her voice cold. “But not to me.”

She turned and left the hall, keeping her spine straight until the doors shut behind her, and only then let her shoulders fall the slightest bit. Being their daughter required performance; being herself required privacy.

I wish they could love without condition. Just understand my perspective. That’s it. Just listen.

In the corridor beyond, Zevora exhaled slowly, her breath a fragile thing in the cold air. She stepped to an arched window, the chill biting at her skin as she looked out over Daesmarch. The streets below were a network of strict lines and ordered chaos—smoke rising from the forges, steam flowing through the chilly air, soldiers drilling in disciplined ranks, merchants and homeless commoners haggling in the crowded city streets.

Somewhere down there, she thought, were the people her father dismissed so easily—the hungry, the lost, the forgotten.

Zevora touched the pendant at her neck, her fingers tracing the silver glyph etched into its surface. “Grant me wisdom,” she whispered to the unseen Artisan in the sky, “and the ability to see what others will not.”

Zevora stood at the threshold of her childhood bedroom, the door creaking softly as it swung open. The walls, once adorned with vibrant posters and maps of distant lands, now stood dull, their colors faded with edges beginning to curl. The bed, a sturdy frame of dark wood, was neatly made, nice and tight, its linens a muted shade of grey that contrasted sharply with the room’s former vibrancy.

On it, a plush bear—its fur slightly faded but its eyes still bright—rested against the pillows. It had been a gift from her mother when she was a child, one of the only symbols of warmth in a house that often felt cold.

She picked it up without thinking. The fur was worn in the exact places she used to grip it during storms, when thunder drowned out even her father’s voice. She held it now not for comfort, but for remembrance.

A small wooden desk sat against one wall, its surface cluttered with remnants of her past: a brass telescope, its lens clouded with age; a stack of well-worn alchemy books, their spines creased and pages yellowed; and a collection of glass vials, some still containing faint traces of shimmering liquids never fully cleaned out. These items, once things for her burgeoning curiosity, now seemed out of place in the sterile environment of the room.

On the windowsill, a single potted plant—a hardy fern—somehow thrived without her living there, its green fronds a testament to resilience. Nearby, a small wooden box, intricately carved with symbols of the Daevor family crest, lay open, revealing a few cherished trinkets: a tarnished brass locket, a faded artist rendition of her parents, and a delicate bracelet of intertwined threads. These were the remnants of a life she had left behind, tokens of a time when the world outside these walls seemed both vast and inviting.

Zevora stepped into the room, her brown leather boots muffled against the worn carpet. She moved to the desk, her fingers brushing over the alchemy books, their spines familiar yet distant.

Zevora breathed in the faint scent of old books and lavender—the last scents of a childhood that had vanished long before she left this place.

As Zevora turned to gather her belongings neatly placed at the side of her bed, she noticed her mother, Natalia, entering the room with a small watering can. She approached the fern, her fingers gently lifting the leaves to water the soil beneath.

“Keeping this old thing alive, are we? I wondered how it was still going after all these years,” Zevora remarked, her voice soft but carrying the weight of distance.

Natalia paused, her gaze meeting Zevora’s. A faint smile tugged at her lips. “Some things are worth the effort,” she replied, her tone warm yet tinged with a hint of bitterness.

Watching her mother tend something delicate felt almost unreal. Natalia had never seemed fragile enough to nurture anything, especially not Zevora. The room, once a place of childhood dreams, now felt like a bridge between their worlds—a space where unspoken words lingered in the air.

“Truly, I didn’t know you still kept this,” Zevora said, her voice barely above a whisper.

Natalia glanced up, her eyes reflecting a mixture of emotions. “I brought it into my bedroom after you left for school. I brought it back in here—I thought it might remind you of home,” she said, her voice carrying a rare tenderness that Zevora hadn’t realized she longed for. “And it reminds me of you.”

Zevora nodded, her throat tightening. She had always believed her mother to be distant, consumed by the family’s ambitions and aesthetics. Yet, in this simple act, she saw a glimpse of the woman who had once held her close, who had nurtured her dreams.

As Natalia finished watering the fern, she set the can down and turned to Zevora. “Are you leaving soon?” she asked, her voice betraying a hint of concern.

Zevora hesitated, then nodded. “I have to. There’s work to do back home. I could only stay the night.”

Natalia stepped forward, her hands reaching out to adjust a stray lock of Zevora’s hair. “Take care of yourself,” she said softly. “Be safe. And remember, no matter where you are, this will always be your home.”

The words should have comforted her. Instead, they wrapped around her like an old blanket—familiar, but lacking the warmth she was looking for.

Zevora smiled faintly. “Thank you, Mother,” she replied, her voice tinged with a little emotion.

With a final glance at the room that had once been her sanctuary, Zevora turned and left, the door closing softly behind her.

The halls of the Daevor estate were quiet, the only sound the soft echo of her footsteps on the marble floors. And as she descended the grand staircase, its ornate railings gleaming in the dim light, the weight of her family’s expectations—especially her mother’s—pressed upon her shoulders. Yet, in that brief exchange, she had found a sliver of the connection she had longed for—a reminder that, despite their differences, there was still love between them.

✦

The cool evening air brushed against Zevora’s face as she stepped out from her parents’ home, down a winding path, and onto the bustling streets of Daesmarch. The city her family built centuries ago, the city she spent the first 16 years of her life in, was a city of dichotomy—a place where the old and the new coexisted in a delicate balance. The streets were a labyrinth of cobblestones, brass, and iron, the buildings towering and imposing, their facades adorned with intricate carvings and wrought-iron balconies.

She paused for a moment, adjusting the strap of her satchel across her chest. Daesmarch was nothing like Etherforge, the booming industrial behemoth she had called home for the last twelve years. Here, everything felt suppressed, ruled over—the smells, the sounds, the disparities.

Steam-powered machinery thrummed beneath the streets, mixing with the ever-present hiss of etheric resin refinery stacks. Knights marched in disciplined lines, their boots hammering out a rhythm that matched the militant heartbeat of the city.

Zevora had always felt that Daesmarch wore its identity too proudly.

The glyphs carved into the façades of buildings pulsed faintly, their energy a testament to the wealth and power of those who could afford such enchantments. But below the shimmering glow of these magical markers lay shadows—streets where the light barely reached, where the echoes of poverty lingered.

As she made her way down the main street, the disparities became impossible to ignore. Merchants hollered from their stalls, peddling wares to commoners who clutched their Coin tightly, while a few Glyphwrights strode through the streets, their long coats pristine and unblemished by the grime that clung to just about everyone else.

She stopped when she saw a small group of children sitting on the corner, no parents in sight, their faces streaked with soot and dirt. One of them, a little girl with hollow cheeks, held out a hand to passing strangers, her voice too soft to be heard over the ruckus.

Zevora’s heart twisted, but she didn’t hesitate; compassion had always reached her faster than caution ever could. Walking closer, she knelt before them, pulling a small cloth-wrapped bundle from her satchel. Inside were three freshly baked rolls she had grabbed on her way out of her parents’ home.

“Here,” she said gently, offering the bundle to the girl. “Make sure to share, alright?”

The girl’s glistening eyes flicked up to meet hers, disbelief etched across her young face. Slowly, she took the bundle and clutched it to her chest, nodding silently.

Zevora felt how small her hands were. Too small. And it struck her, as it always did, that hunger made children look older and younger all at once.

One of the other children whispered something to the little girl, and she turned back to Zevora with a quiet, “Thank you.”

Zevora smiled. “Take care of each other,” she said softly before standing and continuing down the street, her boots clicking against the cobblestones.

The city transformed around her as she walked, its pulse quickening as she neared the train station, more steam moistening the air. The scent of roasted chestnuts mingled with the bite of burning etheric resin, a pungent reminder of the substance that fueled the city’s prosperity. She turned a corner and froze for a moment as she spotted an alleyway bathed in resin smoke.

Two men leaned against the wall, their eyes glassy, their hands trembling as they passed a crude pipe between them. Rez smoking wasn’t uncommon here—it was legalized throughout the provinces a few years back, after all—but it was frowned upon to partake in public, a habit of the desperate or those too lost to care. One of the men noticed her and quickly averted his gaze, pretending to be looking for something he dropped.

Zevora’s brow furrowed as she watched them, the smoke thickening after another puff. She sighed as her fingers brushed a lock of hair behind her right ear. After a moment, she turned away. This was bigger than anything she could fix with a single act of kindness.

The train station was alive with chaos. Steam hissed from brass pipes, mingling with the clatter of luggage carts and the blaring horns of arriving trains. Couriers weaved through the crowd, their arms laden with trunks and parcels, while travelers hurried back and forth, their voices rising above the noise.

Street performers had claimed a corner of the plaza near the ticket booths, their lively brass instruments and drums filling the air with a jazzy, upbeat tune. A crowd had gathered to watch, clapping in time with the rhythm as the performers danced and spun, their energy infectious. Zevora dropped a coin into the open case at their feet as she passed, nodding in appreciation when one of the drummers tipped his hat in thanks.

The disparities between Glyphwrights and commoners were pretty obvious here. The ticket booth stood divided into two distinct sections. On one side, a glyph panel waited for travelers to make it glow. A faint pulse of light would either ignite the ticket stub with an ethereal glow or leave it dull and ordinary—a simple test that determined one’s place on the train.

The other side of the booth catered to commoners, its queues much longer and filled with weary faces. Here, the tickets bore no magical enhancement, just ink stamps denoting basic access to the standard cars.

Zevora lingered near the booths, watching as the two lines flowed at vastly different paces. A young man, likely a commoner judging by his soot-streaked clothes, approached the glyph panel and pressed his palm against it. The glyph didn’t respond. He frowned, muttering something under his breath, and moved half-heartedly to join the commoners’ queue.

The sight left an ache in her chest.

She stepped forward, standing before the glyph-etched panel. The faint sound of magic hummed as the glyph activated instantly, her ticket glowing softly. She accepted it from the attendant, offering a polite smile, but her mind churned with unease.

The train rested on the tracks, a colossal gleaming serpent of iron and brass, its first-class cars adorned with swirling glyphs that shimmered in the evening light. Each one radiated enchantments for protection and comfort—temperature control, noise dampening. Further down the line, the commoners’ cars sat in stark contrast. The windows were smaller, the exteriors lacking the ornate embellishments of the first-class compartments.

She approached the platform but slowed as she noticed a commotion nearby. It drew a small crowd—a shouting match between two men, one in a uniform and the other a commoner clutching a worn satchel.

“Sir, I already paid for this ticket!” the commoner exclaimed, holding up a crumpled piece of paper.

“It’s invalid,” the Knight said flatly, pointing to the dull stub. “You know the rules. Without a proper pass, you can’t board. I’m not going to say it again.”

The man’s face flushed. “But I’ve got a sick wife waiting for me in Riverton! I need to be on this train!”

Zevora’s heart clenched. She moved through the gathering crowd, stepping into the space between the guard and the man. “What’s going on here?” she asked, her voice calm but firm.

The Knight hesitated, glancing at her fine coat. “Miss, this doesn’t concern you. It’s a matter of regulation.”

“Regulations can’t see his wife,” Zevora replied coolly. She turned to the commoner, her gaze softening. “Do you have any Coin left?”

“Not enough for another ticket,” he muttered, looking down.

Without another word, Zevora reached into her satchel, retrieving a handful of Coin. She pressed them into his hand, ignoring the way the guard’s eyes narrowed. “Here,” she said. “Get a new ticket.”

It wasn’t heroism. She simply couldn’t bear the thought of someone suffering within arm’s reach when she had the means to ease it.

The man’s eyes widened. He stammered a thank-you, his voice thick with emotion, before hurrying back to the ticket booth. The guard opened his mouth to protest, but Zevora raised a hand, cutting him off. “If you’re going to enforce the rules,” she said quietly, “do so with a little compassion. You’re human first, then a guard.”

The Knight said nothing, stepping back with a faint scowl.

Why is he even mad? It doesn’t affect him at all…

Zevora boarded the train, stepping into the plush confines of the first-class car. The seats were upholstered in fine leather, their brass fittings polished to a mirror shine. Glyphs glowed faintly along the edges of the windows, filling the space with a gentle warmth. But the luxuries only made her feel heavier, as if she were carrying the weight of the city’s inequalities on her shoulders.

Through the window, she caught a glimpse of a Fenerii—a sleek, fox-like creature with three flowing tails, its grey fur shimmering in the waning light. It pulled a small carriage along the edge of the platform, its graceful stride mesmerizing.

She smiled despite herself. Simple things—small wonders—still had the power to stir her heart. Zevora held on to that tightly; it was the one part of her the world hadn’t managed to harden.

Sometimes I wish I became a Warden just to have one of those…

As the train lurched forward, she leaned back in her seat, her thoughts lingering on the faces she’d seen—the children, the addicts, the man desperate to reach his wife. As Daesmarch receded in the window’s reflection, Zevora felt the ache she always carried—the longing for a version of home that had never quite existed.

Etherforge awaited her, and as always on that long ride home, she wondered whether her journey was leading her somewhere new—or simply away from everything she could not mend.
